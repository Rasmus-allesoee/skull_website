## Before you begin {#before-you-begin}

This guide concerns fresh or frozen animal remains for a dry skull collection. Archaeological, chemically preserved or fragile material needs separate consideration. Preparation can affect future research value; record treatments and choose the method accordingly. [cite:hendry]

<Aside title="Handling and workspace">

Do not collect or process animals suspected of infectious disease. Avoid contact with sick or unexpectedly dead birds and other potentially infected wildlife; follow the relevant wildlife authority’s reporting advice. Gloves alone do not make an infected carcass safe. [cite:bird-flu]

Use dedicated tools and containers, eye protection against splashes, suitable gloves, and a washable work area separate from food, children, pets and finished specimens. Wash hands after handling. Never use smell, whiteness, freezing or ordinary preparation as proof of disinfection. Check the product label and safety data sheet before each chemical step.

</Aside>

Confirm permission to possess the animal before starting; rules depend on species and location. Plan disposal with your local waste authority, including tissue, biologically contaminated water and used chemicals. This guide does not authorize burial or discharge into soil, drains or waterways. Keep different waste streams separate and labelled rather than combining leftovers.

Keep a tray, fine sieve or mesh bag, forceps, a small sharp knife, soft brush, labels, and separate containers for loose parts ready. Photograph the unprepared head and its teeth. Assign one label to each specimen and carry that label through every bath. Existing breaks and missing teeth are worth recording before cleaning changes the evidence.

## 1. Preparation for defleshing {#preparation}

The aim is access to the tissues, without damage to the skull. How much preliminary trimming is useful depends on the method: a water bath can finish work that would be awkward with a knife; a beetle colony benefits from a carefully trimmed head. Preserve the parts you actually intend to retain.

### Separating the head: C1 and C2 {#separation}

The atlas (C1) is the first neck vertebra, adjoining the skull; the axis (C2) lies behind it. Work at an exposed joint rather than trying to cut through a vertebra. Support the head on a stable surface, cut surrounding soft tissue in small controlled passes, and move the joint gently to see where it opens. Keep the blade away from the supporting hand.

If the skull–atlas joint cannot be seen clearly, leave a short piece of neck attached for maceration and separate it after the tissues loosen. This is preferable to levering a blade against the skull or twisting until something breaks. Do not force the occipital region or a delicate skull base.

<Aside title="From my collection · separating and skinning">

I have found the atlas–axis region awkward and have sometimes relied on force after cutting into the joint. I have not settled on an ideal technique. For this guide, the more controlled option is to expose the joint or let maceration finish the separation.

For skinning, I start with a cut along the top of the head, from the back toward the nose, then use my fingers to pull the skin toward the mouth. Small cuts help release attachments as I go. Around the lips and the underside of the jaw, I work in short sections; I do not have one consistent technique for every head.

</Aside>

### Removing the skin {#skinning}

Cut through the skin only, lift an edge, and use gentle traction to reveal the next attachment. Cut the attachment you can see instead of scraping blindly across bone. Work around the eyes, ears, lips and jaw angle with particular care. Keep the nasal opening and its thin internal bones clear of the knife. There is no need to make the skull visibly clean at this stage.

### How much tissue should you trim? {#trimming}

For my maceration preparations, skinning alone can be enough. On medium and large carnivores I also remove the bulky temporalis muscles and accessible soft cartilage. I generally leave the tongue, eyes and brain for the bath rather than spending time on difficult dissection. That is a practical choice, not a requirement to leave them in every method.

Removing easily reached muscle reduces what the bath or colony must process; trying to remove every tendon can cost time and damage delicate surfaces. Museum preparation guidance emphasizes careful preliminary dissection and retaining small skeletal elements such as the hyoid apparatus. If you want these, separate and label them rather than discarding the throat tissues indiscriminately. [cite:harris]

Skin is removed, unnecessary bulk is trimmed without scoring bone, and every retained part has a specimen label. Take another photograph before the head enters the chosen treatment.

</Aside>

## 2. Defleshing: choose a method {#defleshing}

All five routes remove soft tissue, with different equipment and recovery risks. The table gives planning scales, not completion promises. Maceration is the collector’s main method. Burial can stain bone; prolonged boiling can damage it. [cite:hendry]

| Method             | Planning scale                               | Advantages                                            | Tradeoffs                                                  |
| ------------------ | -------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Water maceration   | Weeks; cold conditions may mean months       | Simple equipment; inspectable bath; little knife work | Odour, contaminated liquid, loose teeth and parts          |
| Dermestid beetles  | Days to weeks; colony-dependent              | Good access to delicate structures                    | Colony care, containment and pest isolation                |
| Burial             | Months or longer; site-dependent             | Little active equipment                               | Poor monitoring, staining, loss and root intrusion         |
| Above-ground decay | Weeks to months or longer; weather-dependent | Insects can reach tissue                              | Scavengers, drying, exposure and scattered parts           |
| Controlled heat    | Hours of treatment plus cleanup              | Fast tissue loosening                                 | Greater risk to teeth and fragile bone; not a rolling boil |

### Water maceration {#maceration}

Submerge the skinned head completely in water in a dedicated, labelled container. Use enough water to cover the highest point and leave headroom for displaced liquid and gas. A fine mesh bag or basket within the bucket makes recovery easier; choose a mesh finer than the smallest tooth you want to keep. Inspect the seams and closure. No bag guarantees recovery of every tiny element.

Keep the container in a secure, ventilated work area. Use a loosely fitted or properly vented cover to exclude animals and reduce splashes; do not snap a gas-producing bath airtight. There is no recommended schedule for periodically opening an airtight lid: avoid building pressure in the first place. Briefly opening a lid is also not a reliable way to oxygenate the whole bath.

#### Temperature and timing {#maceration-timing}

My approximate fox-skull observations are more than three months around 10 °C outdoors, sometimes with persistent tendons, versus roughly two to four weeks around 20 °C. These are personal estimates, not controlled trials, and do not guarantee complete removal of wax or connective tissue. A larger head, more retained tissue or fluctuating temperature changes the result.

Use the measured water temperature in your notes, not just the air temperature. A stable mild environment is easier to compare between batches than a bucket alternately chilled at night and heated by sunlight. This guide does not set a universal optimum or prescribe an unattended heater: the available sources and collector observations do not establish one for every skull.

#### Water changes and recovery {#water-changes}

In my workflow, I change about 80% of the initially bloody water after five to seven days, then inspect periodically until the tissue has released. Retaining some old water carries existing microorganisms into the fresh bath, but there is no demonstrated requirement for exactly 20% old water. Do not add disinfectant or peroxide to a bath intended for biological maceration.

At each change, pour slowly through a fine sieve into a second dedicated container so all liquid is captured for appropriate disposal. Keep the skull supported, leave sediment in the bucket until inspected, and search it over a contrasting tray. Do not empty it onto the ground: that makes tiny teeth difficult to recover and does not establish acceptable disposal.

Remove teeth only when they are already loose enough to lift without force. Keep left and right tooth groups and the jaw halves separated and labelled. Inspect behind the teeth, inside the braincase and along the lower jaw for tissue. Gentle low-pressure rinsing and fine forceps help reach recesses; avoid a jet through the delicate nasal bones. [cite:alaska]

<Figure asset="maceration" />

<Details title="Troubleshooting: dark bone, wax and stubborn tendons">

Blackening is not, by itself, proof of one particular microbe or oxygen condition. Do not promise that airing the lid will prevent it or that peroxide will remove every stain. First establish whether there is a loose surface deposit, retained tissue or staining within the bone. Record it before further treatment.

Adipocere, or corpse wax, forms through changes to fats during decomposition. It can resist cleaning; a lid’s air supply alone does not explain its presence. [cite:hendry]

For a waxy deposit, try gentle mechanical removal after soaking in a suitable warm aqueous detergent bath. A small student study found detergent soaking more useful than peroxide for adipocere removal; it is limited evidence, not a universal protocol. Stop if the bone surface begins to shed. [cite:adipocere]

For tendons that still resist, return to maceration rather than pulling hard. In my preparations, the mandibular foramen can retain wax-like debris. Work under water over a recovery tray: use a gentle rinse and, only where the channel is visible and accessible, a fine blunt probe. Do not drill, enlarge the opening or force a sharp wire through resistance.

</Details>

### Dermestid beetles {#beetles}

These are dermestid beetles, not desmid beetles. Museum colonies clean tissue from many vertebrate groups, including delicate skeletons; maintaining the colony environment is part of the method. [cite:amnh-beetles]

1. Arrange access to an established colony or a preparator. Check what specimen condition the colony keeper accepts before delivering material.
2. Skin and trim the head, retaining the required small bones. Keep preservatives, insecticides and whitening chemicals away from the colony.
3. Place the prepared material on a labelled recovery tray in the escape-resistant colony enclosure. Inspect progress regularly and remove the skull when the required tissue has been eaten.
4. Check cavities for remaining tissue and insects, recover loose parts, and complete the keeper’s pest-isolation procedure before taking material near a finished collection. Degreasing may still be needed.

For occasional skulls, using a maintained colony is usually a more practical choice than creating a new one. Colony capacity and feeding activity determine the schedule; a duration quoted for one setup is not a service guarantee for another.

### Burial {#burial}

Keep one specimen in a durable fine-mesh recovery bag inside a rigid, recoverable perforated container. Mark and record the location, retain a duplicate label, and arrange protection from digging animals. Soil organisms need access, but loose teeth should remain contained. Only use a site where the landowner and applicable rules permit this method.

Excavate the whole container onto a tray, not just the visible skull. Check for roots, fragments and loose teeth before discarding surrounding material. If tissue remains, reassess the method rather than repeatedly digging without a plan. Burial trades low active effort for poor visibility; reserve it for material where staining and loss would be acceptable.

### Leaving material above ground {#above-ground}

Use a securely anchored rigid mesh cage with a closed base, a fine recovery tray and a closure animals cannot lift. Tying a head to a stake is not equivalent containment. Allow insect access while preventing a scavenger from pulling the head through the mesh; inspect after bad weather or disturbance.

A very dry head may mummify instead of cleaning efficiently. Transfer persistent tissue to maceration if the exposure method stalls. Recover all debris over a tray and check the site carefully. This method needs a suitable secure location and tolerance for odour; it is not a reason to leave unsecured remains in shared outdoor space.

### Boiling and controlled simmering {#heat}

Avoid a rolling boil. Alaska’s wildlife-agency guide describes controlled heating around 160 °F, approximately 71 °C, and warns that excessive heat exposure can crack teeth and soften bone. It gives roughly 40–60 minutes for some small skulls and much longer for large ones; these examples are not safe fixed timers for every specimen. [cite:alaska]

If choosing heat, thoroughly trim the head, use a dedicated temperature-monitored vessel, keep the skull submerged and check frequently. Remove loosened tissue gently between short treatments. Stop as soon as it releases; do not keep cooking to eliminate every last tendon. Follow with appropriate grease removal and recovery of loose teeth. Fragile, juvenile or scientifically valuable skulls are poor candidates for this shortcut.

<Aside title="Ready for degreasing">

No visible muscle, skin or persistent connective tissue remains. Cavities have been inspected and rinsed, and the skull, teeth and other retained parts are accounted for. Whitening cannot substitute for this check.

</Aside>

## 3. Degreasing {#degreasing}

Flesh-free bone can retain grease. Degreasing addresses residual fats; whitening is a separate treatment. Museum literature describes solvent and alkaline methods with differing risks. [cite:hendry]

### Start with an aqueous detergent bath {#detergent}

For a modest home collection, begin with a clear, uncoloured dishwashing detergent suitable for hand washing, or a documented aqueous laboratory cleaner. Avoid added bleach, abrasive cleaners and strongly coloured or perfumed formulations. A laboratory product’s stated working dilution is more reproducible than an unspecified squirt of household soap; dishwashing products are not interchangeable concentrations.

1. Rinse away maceration residue before starting. Keep loose teeth in their labelled container or fine basket.
2. Cover the skull in clean water and add detergent at the product’s hand-washing dilution, or follow the laboratory cleaner’s instructions. Record product, amount, water volume and temperature.
3. Keep the bath mild and stable. Do not apply the temperature for a different cleaner or industrial degreasing system to your product.
4. Replace the entire solution when it becomes visibly oily, heavily cloudy or laden with residue. Rinse off loosened deposits before refilling. Cloudiness alone is not a chemical measurement of remaining grease.
5. After progress slows, rinse thoroughly and let the skull dry before evaluating the surface. Return it to a fresh bath if oily or translucent patches recur.

<Figure asset="degreasing" />

### Comparing degreasers {#degreaser-comparison}

There is no established fastest product for every species. The recommendation here favours a manageable aqueous starting point; moving to a solvent is a change in equipment and safety requirements, not just a stronger version of dish soap.

| Agent                      | Useful role                               | Limitations                                              | Working dilution                                       |
| -------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Clear dish detergent       | Accessible first bath                     | Slow; formulation varies; rinse residues well            | Product’s hand-washing instructions; record the amount |
| Aqueous laboratory cleaner | Reproducible formulation and instructions | Compatibility must be checked for bone                   | Manufacturer’s specified working solution              |
| Aqueous ammonia            | Specialist alkaline option                | Irritating vapour and splash hazard                      | Requires a concentration-specific protocol and SDS     |
| Acetone                    | Specialist solvent for persistent fats    | Highly flammable; equipment, glove and waste constraints | No generic home-bath dilution supplied                 |

### Ammonia: a separate controlled process {#ammonia-method}

The relevant product is aqueous ammonia, sometimes called ammonium hydroxide, not an arbitrary ammonium salt. It can injure eyes and skin and irritate breathing passages. Never mix it with chlorine bleach. Consult the concentration-specific SDS, provide appropriate ventilation and chemical protection, and keep the bath labelled and separate from other treatments. [cite:ammonia]

Only proceed with a bone-compatible protocol for the actual product concentration. Monitor the surface, then rinse thoroughly between processes. This guide does not transplant concentrated historical museum recipes into an unsupervised home bath.

### Acetone: a specialist option {#acetone-method}

Acetone is highly flammable even at ordinary room temperature, and vapour can cause irritation and dizziness. Never heat it or place it near flames, sparks, electrical heaters or peroxide. A dust mask does not control solvent vapour. Use solvent-rated equipment, ventilation, eye protection and gloves selected from the actual SDS; disposable gloves are not automatically suitable. [cite:acetone]

A trained preparator should choose the solvent-compatible vessel, lid, transfer method and waste route. Do not substitute a reused food bucket, improvised bag, or nail-polish remover with unknown additives. Rinse and dry as required by the chosen protocol before any separate whitening treatment; never mix treatment liquids.

<Details title="How long, which animals, and when is it finished?">

Budget repeated inspections rather than a species-based deadline. A small skull with little residual fat may finish sooner than a larger or persistently greasy specimen, but size alone is not a reliable predictor. Allow weeks and be prepared for a longer treatment; this is a planning allowance, not a validated species timetable.

There is no verified young-versus-old rule in the sources reviewed for this guide. Juvenile skulls also demand care because their elements can separate; age is not a reason to use stronger chemicals. The collector has not supplied controlled species-by-species degreasing records, so exact durations would imply evidence we do not have.

Use several checks together: a rinsed, fully dry surface without oily patches; no fresh grease appearing during a rest period; and little new contamination of a fresh bath. Do not chase every coloured mark as grease: mineral stains and natural colour can persist. Keep the observation date so later seepage can be recognized.

</Details>

<Aside title="Ready for whitening or assembly">

After rinsing and dry inspection, the skull has no recurring oily patches or retained detergent. If the natural colour is acceptable, skip whitening and proceed to assembly.

</Aside>

## 4. Whitening: optional, not a cleaning shortcut {#whitening}

Whitening is optional. Preserve satisfactory natural colour, especially for research material. Conservation literature cautions that peroxide can damage bone. [cite:hendry]

### A plain hydrogen peroxide bath {#peroxide-bath}

Choose plain aqueous hydrogen peroxide with a known concentration. For optional whitening of sound bone, 3% is a conservative starting concentration used in published skeletal preparation, rather than starting at 12%. Even this is not a guarantee against damage. [cite:harris]

Use a compatible clean plastic container, enough liquid to contact the intended surface, and an extra tray to catch overflow. Work at room temperature out of direct sun. Inspect frequently from the start and stop once the result is adequate. Rinse thoroughly with clean water and let the skull dry naturally before deciding whether another treatment is justified.

There is no validated concentration-to-hours conversion for these varied skulls. Do not prescribe “overnight at 12%” or leave a bath unattended for days. Fragility, previous treatment, retained tissue and product formulation all change the outcome. Stop if the surface becomes chalky, roughened or starts shedding.

<Aside title="Peroxide protection and pressure">

Prevent skin and eye contact; wear splash protection and chemical-compatible gloves. Peroxide is an oxidizer: keep it separate from acetone and other organic solvents, incompatible metals and cleaning products. If splashed, flush with water promptly and follow the SDS and local poison-service advice. [cite:peroxide]

Decomposition releases oxygen; heat and light accelerate it. Do not seal a treatment container or bag, even with a dilute bath. External air is not needed to supply the reaction’s oxygen. The cited ILO card concerns concentrated peroxide and supports this chemistry, not permission to use its concentration here. [cite:icsc]

</Aside>

### The collector’s cream developer {#cream-developer}

I use Fanola 40 Vol Perfumed Cream Developer, labelled 12%, diluted with water. The retailer describes a hair-development product; that is not a bone-conservation approval. I have found it inexpensive, but price and formulation can change, so this guide does not publish a fixed current price. [cite:fanola]

My note says “about 80% water”. If that means four parts water to one part 12% developer, the nominal arithmetic is about 2.4%; if it means adding water equal to 80% of the developer volume, it is about 6.7%. These are different mixtures. Cream density, formulation and whether a percentage is by mass or volume also matter: neither calculation establishes the measured strength of my previous baths.

For a reproducible record, write the actual product and measured quantities. Plain aqueous peroxide is easier to characterize than a cream emulsion. A hair developer contains formulation ingredients beyond peroxide and water, so rinse residues thoroughly and do not assume all developers behave alike. My mixture can foam considerably; leave generous headroom and use a catch tray.

<Figure asset="whitening" />

| Choice                           | Benefit                                               | Caution                                                    |
| -------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| No whitening                     | Preserves natural colour and avoids another exposure  | Does not remove existing stains                            |
| Plain 3% aqueous peroxide        | Known starting concentration; straightforward rinsing | Monitor for damage; no universal treatment time            |
| Stronger peroxide, including 12% | More concentrated stock                               | Greater handling burden; not the recommended starting bath |
| Cream developer                  | Clings to exposed surfaces                            | Extra ingredients; harder to rinse and quantify            |
| Chlorine bleach                  | Not recommended                                       | Harsh on bone; do not substitute for peroxide              |

Avoid household chlorine bleach (sodium hypochlorite): wildlife-agency guidance specifically cautions against it for skulls. Do not combine it with ammonia, peroxide or other cleaners. [cite:alaska]

<Details title="Use less peroxide without chasing a stronger bath">

First finish defleshing and degreasing so peroxide is not consumed by avoidable residues. Choose the smallest compatible tub that comfortably fits the skull, rotate only if you can keep all intended surfaces treated evenly, and keep separate parts in a smaller vessel.

An open compatible plastic liner supported inside a rigid catch tray can reduce unused volume, but teeth may puncture it. Never zip or tie it closed. A brushed cream treatment uses less liquid but can whiten unevenly and leave material in recesses. Do not improvise a thickening paste with household powders; use only a documented bone-compatible formulation. Plain immersion is the simpler reproducible option here.

</Details>

## 5. Teeth, mandibles and final care {#assembly}

Finish every wet process and let the parts dry before attaching anything. First arrange and photograph all loose teeth next to their probable positions. A clean specimen with deliberately removable parts can be more useful for study than one permanently glued into a display pose.

<Figure asset="assembly" />

### Replacing teeth {#teeth}

Dry-fit every tooth without force. Match the root shape, side and neighbouring teeth; a tooth that stops high or rotates strangely may be in the wrong socket. Do not grind it or enlarge the socket to make it fit. If placement is uncertain, keep it labelled separately until identified.

I use super glue and have applied it over roughly half of the root. For a controlled repair, start with the smallest amount that holds rather than treating that coverage as a target. Apply to a hidden contact area, keep the visible crown clean, seat the tooth in the rehearsed orientation, and support it while the adhesive sets. Avoid filling the socket with excess glue.

### Choosing an adhesive {#adhesive-choice}

AMNH distinguishes solvent-setting adhesives such as Paraloid B-72 from reactive products such as cyanoacrylate and epoxy; they differ in working and removal properties. Its guidance concerns fossils, so selecting a product for fresh bone still needs material-specific judgment. [cite:adhesives]

| Option                                      | Where it helps                                                | Main tradeoff                                                  |
| ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Keep parts separate                         | Study, uncertain placement, future treatment                  | Needs labelled, fitted storage                                 |
| Conservation adhesive such as Paraloid B-72 | Planned, retreatable joins under an appropriate protocol      | Solvent handling and careful formulation                       |
| White glue                                  | Simple small display joins; mentioned in the Alaska guide     | Formulations and ageing differ; not all are conservation grade |
| Super glue                                  | Collector’s fast-setting method for sound, well-fitting parts | Little adjustment time; excess is difficult to correct         |

For an irreplaceable or fragile specimen, seek conservation advice before gluing. Do not coat the entire skull simply to make it shiny or hide grease. Leave a record of the product and attachment points.

### Aligning the mandibles {#mandibles}

The join between left and right lower-jaw halves is the mandibular symphysis, at the front; the temporomandibular joints connect the jaw to the skull behind. Rehearse the complete fit before adding adhesive. Check both joint positions and how the tooth rows meet, rather than aligning only the front seam.

I glue the separated halves at their front join. With badgers, I have found the jaw joints can trap the condyles: it may be necessary to seat each half separately before joining them. Treat this as a collector’s observation, not a reason to force every badger jaw into place. If it will not fit gently, leave the halves separate and investigate. Do not glue the moving jaw joints to the skull merely to hold a pose.

### Drying, storage and documentation {#storage}

Dry in a supported position at room temperature, away from direct heat. Protect teeth and thin projections from pressure. Avoid abrupt changes in temperature and humidity, direct sun and heat sources; a suitable enclosure helps keep dust and environmental fluctuations down. Handle the dry skull with clean hands and support it from below. [cite:care]

Before enclosing it, check recesses for moisture and review the dry surface for grease or residue. Store separated teeth and bones in labelled compartments with the same specimen identifier. Inspect later for oil, new cracks, loose joins or pest activity. Return to the relevant phase only when the problem is identified.

Record the defleshing method, bath dates and temperatures, products and quantities, water changes, whitening exposure, adhesive, losses and repairs. Photograph the finished skull from consistent views before making measurements. These observations are more useful to a future preparator than a simple note saying “cleaned”.
