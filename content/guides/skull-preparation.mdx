## 0. Before you begin {#before-you-begin}

If you have just found a dead animal, start here: make sure you may keep it, avoid handling anything that looks sick or died unexpectedly, put on gloves, and either begin soon or freeze the head in a leakproof bag. Freezing pauses decomposition and buys you time; it does not disinfect the animal. Alaska’s wildlife guide likewise recommends freezing a skull that cannot be cleaned promptly. [cite:alaska]

<Aside title="Four checks before bringing it home">

1. Check the rules for that species and location before you collect it. Protected wildlife, roadkill and hunted animals are not treated the same everywhere.
2. Leave animals suspected of infectious disease alone. Current public-health advice specifically warns against contact with sick or dead wild birds where bird flu may be present. [cite:bird-flu]
3. Use gloves, dedicated tools and a washable work area away from food, children, pets and finished skulls. Protect your eyes during splashy steps and wash your hands afterwards.
4. Decide how you will handle tissue, dirty water and used chemicals before filling a bucket. Never mix leftover treatments.

</Aside>

The complete process is defleshing, degreasing, optional whitening, and drying or assembly. Do not jump straight to peroxide: it changes colour, but it does not replace removal of flesh and grease. Community and museum guides agree on that order. [cite:reddit] [cite:nps]

| Starting condition                          | Best place to start                                                                     | What to expect                                                   |
| ------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Fresh or frozen head                        | Skin it, then choose maceration, beetles, burial, above-ground decay or controlled heat | More trimming means less smell and a shorter defleshing stage    |
| Partly decomposed head                      | Contain it and continue with maceration, burial or above-ground decay                   | Loose teeth and small bones may already be present               |
| Dry or mummified head                       | Rehydrate in plain water, then macerate or use beetles                                  | Dry tissue can take days to soften before decomposition restarts |
| Bare skull with yellow or translucent areas | Begin at degreasing                                                                     | Let it dry between baths so hidden grease becomes visible        |
| Clean, grease-free skull                    | Whiten only if you want a paler colour                                                  | Natural cream or tan bone is not a failed result                 |

## 1. Preparation for defleshing {#preparation}

Separating the head and removing the skin are common to every method. After that, the amount of tissue to remove depends on how you plan to deflesh it.

### Separating the head: find the joint {#separation}

The atlas, or C1, is the first neck vertebra and sits directly against the skull. The axis, or C2, is behind it. Bend and rotate the neck gently to feel where the skull–atlas joint moves. Cut the muscle around that joint in short passes until you can see or feel the gap, then cut through the remaining connective tissue. Do not try to saw through a vertebra.

I still find this one of the awkward parts. If I cannot expose the joint cleanly, I leave a short section of neck attached and let maceration loosen it. That is easier than damaging the back of the skull while trying to force a knife into a joint I cannot see.

### Removing the skin {#skinning}

I begin with a cut along the top of the head from the back towards the nose. I lift the skin with my fingers, pull it towards the mouth and make small cuts only where it remains attached. Around the lips and under the lower jaw, short controlled cuts work better than trying to remove everything in one piece.

Keep the blade angled into the soft tissue rather than towards the bone. Be especially careful around the eye sockets, the thin nasal opening and any antler or horn bases. The skull does not have to look clean yet; the point is to remove the hide so the next method can reach the tissue.

<Figure asset="skinned-head" />

### Removing tissue for your chosen method {#tissue-removal}

Open only the method you intend to use. A water bath can deal with tissue that would be tedious to dissect, while a beetle colony works best when the head is much more carefully prepared.

<Disclosure id="prep-maceration" level="4" title="For water maceration">

Skinning is usually enough for a small or medium head. On larger heads I also cut away the thick cheek and temporalis muscles because this shortens the process and reduces the initial mess. I normally leave the eyes, tongue and brain for the bath.

Do not spend an hour scraping every tendon from the bone. Remove what is easy to reach without cutting into the skull; maceration is good at the awkward work.

</Disclosure>

<Disclosure id="prep-beetles" level="4" title="For dermestid beetles">

Remove the skin, eyes, tongue, brain, viscera and the large masses of muscle. Leave a thin layer of fresh tissue so the beetles have something to eat, but do not put a wet, dripping head into the colony. The published Dermestes protocol removes these bulky tissues before cleaning. [cite:dermestid]

If you want tiny elements such as the hyoid bones, find and retain them during trimming. Small anatomical parts are easily discarded with throat tissue. [cite:harris]

</Disclosure>

<Disclosure id="prep-burial" level="4" title="For burial">

Skin the head and remove large, easy pieces of muscle if you can tolerate the work. Less tissue means a shorter burial, but a head can still decompose if you leave more attached. Put small skulls or loose parts in a fine mesh bag or a wide-mouthed container only if easier recovery matters to you; these are useful options, not requirements.

</Disclosure>

<Disclosure id="prep-above" level="4" title="For above-ground decay">

You may leave more tissue than for maceration or beetles. Skinning exposes the flesh to flies and other insects, but leaving some skin can slow drying in a hot climate. Whatever you choose, the carcass must fit inside a cage that animals cannot drag through, overturn or dig under.

</Disclosure>

<Disclosure id="prep-heat" level="4" title="For controlled simmering">

Remove the skin and as much flesh as is practical, but leave enough tissue to shield the bone while it heats. This method is for robust, usually larger mammal skulls. Do not use it for birds, fish, very small mammals or young skulls that may fall apart when their connective tissue softens. [cite:reddit]

</Disclosure>

## 2. Defleshing: choose a method {#defleshing}

Maceration is my main method because it needs little specialist equipment and reaches areas that are difficult to clean by hand. Each alternative has a situation where it makes sense.

| Method               | Rough time                                                                             | Why choose it                                       | Main drawbacks                                                           |
| -------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Water maceration     | About 2–4 weeks when warm; cold baths can take months                                  | Cheap, thorough and little knife work               | Strong odour, dirty water and loose teeth                                |
| Dermestid beetles    | Often 3–10 days for small fresh specimens; 20–30 days for larger or difficult material | Excellent for delicate or articulated skeletons     | A colony needs heat, feeding, containment and pest control               |
| Burial               | Usually months                                                                         | Low day-to-day effort and useful for larger remains | Slow, hard to inspect, and may stain or lose parts                       |
| Above-ground decay   | Weeks to months in suitable weather                                                    | Insects do most of the work                         | Scavengers, flies, smell and mummification in dry weather                |
| Controlled simmering | Roughly an hour for some small mammal heads; many hours for large heads                | Fast when time matters                              | Heat can trap grease, crack teeth, soften bone and separate young skulls |

<Disclosure id="maceration" level="3" title="Water maceration">

Maceration is simply decomposition in water. Put the skinned head in a dedicated bucket, cover it completely with plain water and leave enough empty space at the top for foam and gas. A lid keeps animals and rain out and controls some smell.

In winter I sometimes bring the buckets indoors and use a tight-fitting lid because the smell would otherwise be unbearable. I periodically carry the bucket outside, set it down, and open it slowly for a few minutes to release pressure and exchange some air. Never open a swollen or pressurised container towards your face. A loose or vented lid is simpler wherever odour control allows it.

Do not add peroxide, disinfectant or antibacterial dish soap. Maceration depends on microorganisms breaking down the tissue, and those products work against the process. A small amount of ordinary old maceration water can help start a new bath, but fresh tissue will also establish its own active bath. [cite:reddit]

| Conditions      | A useful expectation                                                                                                 |
| --------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Around 20–25 °C | Small and medium mammal skulls may be ready in roughly 2–4 weeks; very fleshy or large heads take longer             |
| Around 10 °C    | Expect months rather than weeks; tendons and waxy deposits may persist                                               |
| Near freezing   | Decomposition nearly stops until the bath warms again                                                                |
| Frozen solid    | The process pauses. Thaw the whole bucket gradually before inspecting; do not pour boiling water onto a frozen skull |

My fox skulls have taken more than three months outdoors at roughly 10 °C, sometimes with tendons still attached. At around 20 °C, similar skulls have usually taken roughly two to four weeks. Treat these as ballpark observations: tissue load, skull size and changing weather matter as much as the number on a thermometer.

I usually replace most of the initially bloody water after about five to seven days. I leave roughly 10–30% of the old liquid in the bucket, then top it up with fresh water. Later I change the water less often, because every complete change removes part of the active microbial community. BoneLust likewise recommends letting a new bath establish before changing it repeatedly. [cite:bonelust]

Pour slowly through a fine sieve so loose teeth cannot disappear with the water. Check the sediment at the bottom before discarding anything. Teeth normally loosen as the gums disappear; let them fall out rather than forcing them.

<Aside title="Beginner tip · keep loose teeth in order">

If tooth placement is unfamiliar, keep the upper and lower teeth separate and divide them into left and right groups as they come loose. I do not usually need to do this because the root and socket shapes make the position clear, but it can save a beginner a frustrating puzzle later.

</Aside>

The main process is finished when no flesh remains on the outside of the skull. The brain often needs a final check: it can remain as a soft white or grey mass inside the cranium after the rest looks clean. I point the foramen magnum down, shake out what will release, then rinse into the braincase until the loose material is gone.

Rinse the nasal cavity gently as well. The turbinates are very thin scrolls of bone and can trap debris; use a low-pressure stream rather than a pressure washer. Alaska’s guide also recommends repeated flushing through the braincase and nasal cavity. [cite:alaska]

<Figure asset="maceration" />

<Aside title="Troubleshooting maceration">

- Dark or strangely coloured bone: let it dry before judging it. Some staining fades after degreasing or peroxide, while some remains. Colour alone does not mean the skull is ruined.
- White, waxy material: this may be adipocere, or corpse wax. Warm detergent, time and gentle mechanical removal are usually more useful than trying to whiten over it. [cite:hendry] [cite:adipocere]
- Persistent tendons: continue maceration. Cold is the usual reason a bath appears to stop.
- Waxy debris in the lower jaw: I sometimes find it inside the mandibular foramen. Rinse the channel under water and use a fine blunt probe or move a thin steel wire in and out to loosen the waxy gunk. It can be quite tricky to fully clean!
- Mummified tissue: soak it in plain water for several days to rehydrate it, then continue maceration. Both Jake’s Bones and the r/bonecollecting guide describe rehydration as the practical first step. [cite:jakes] [cite:reddit]

</Aside>

</Disclosure>

<Disclosure id="beetles" level="3" title="Dermestid beetles">

I have not used dermestid beetles myself, so the small practical details here come from published colony protocols and experienced keepers rather than my own preparations.

Dermestids are popular because their larvae can remove tissue while leaving delicate bones and some articulations together. They are especially useful for fish, birds, reptiles, small mammals and young animals that may disarticulate in water. They are living museum pests, however, so escape control matters as much as feeding them. [cite:dermestid] [cite:amnh-beetles]

1. Use a smooth-sided, escape-resistant enclosure with a tightly fitted ventilated lid. Keep it well away from finished skulls, hides, feathers, textiles and stored food.
2. Keep the colony warm and dark. The reviewed protocol reports roughly 23–29 °C and about 52% relative humidity; excessive damp encourages mould, while cold slows or collapses the colony. [cite:dermestid]
3. Place the trimmed head on a shallow tray. Do not add material treated with insecticide, peroxide or other chemicals.
4. Check small skulls daily. An active colony may clean a fresh small skull in 3–10 days; large, dry or previously treated material can take 20–30 days or longer.
5. Remove the skull when the tissue is gone and before thin bone or cartilage is eaten. Check the braincase and other cavities for larvae and eggs.
6. Isolate the cleaned skull from the collection and follow a proven pest-kill step before storage. The National Park Service describes freezing at −18 to −20 °C for 72 hours as one option. [cite:nps]

The colony still leaves fat in the bone, so move on to degreasing. Maintaining dermestids for one occasional skull is a large commitment: the colony needs regular food, humidity control, ventilation, cleaning and an escape plan even when no specimen is ready.

</Disclosure>

<Disclosure id="burial" level="3" title="Burial">

Burial needs little attention once set up, but it is slow and can stain bone. Choose dark, biologically active soil that stays slightly moist and drains. Heavy waterlogged clay can exclude air and hold stagnant water; very dry sand may preserve or mummify tissue instead of decomposing it. Community guides report several months for larger remains. [cite:reddit] [cite:baccyflap]

1. Dig roughly 60–70 cm deep for an ordinary head. Go deeper in loose sandy soil or where scavengers are active.
2. Optional but useful: wrap a small specimen in fine mesh, place it in a wide perforated plant pot, or lay wire mesh below it. These make teeth easier to recover while still allowing soil organisms to enter.
3. Backfill firmly. Put heavy rocks or rigid mesh over the filled hole so foxes, badgers and dogs cannot dig directly down to the specimen.
4. Mark the location clearly. A label matters mainly when several specimens are close together; for one burial, a durable marker is usually enough.
5. Wait at least a few months before the first careful check. Large heads and cold soil can need much longer.
6. When ready, remove the upper soil with a spade and excavate the last layer by hand over a tray or sheet. Sieve the surrounding soil for teeth and small fragments.

Roots may enter the skull and fine soil can lodge in cavities. Rinse gently after recovery. If tissue remains, rebury it for longer or transfer it to maceration.

</Disclosure>

<Disclosure id="above-ground" level="3" title="Leaving material above ground">

Above-ground decay can be quick in warm, damp weather because flies and other insects have direct access. It can also fail spectacularly if a scavenger takes the carcass or dry weather turns it into leather.

I use the metal top of an old rodent cage. I put a heavy weight on it, but that is not a guarantee: a whole badger has still been moved several metres, and animals have dug underneath the edge. Peg or wire the cage to the ground, weight it, and add a buried skirt of rigid mesh (predator apron) around the perimeter where digging is likely. The openings must admit insects but be too small for an animal to pull the head through.

In dry weather, lay an opaque board or other rain-safe cover over part of the cage to create shade and retain moisture without blocking insect access. Water the carcass when it starts to dry. Once it mummifies, decomposition slows dramatically and may not restart until the tissue is rehydrated. Community experience similarly recommends moisture and shade where drying is a problem. [cite:reddit]

Expect smell and flies. Put the cage where it can be checked after storms and cannot affect neighbours, pets or shared outdoor space. Recover everything over a tray when the bone is exposed, then rinse and inspect the braincase and nasal cavity.

</Disclosure>

<Disclosure id="heat" level="3" title="Boiling and controlled simmering">

Never use a rolling boil. Heat loosens tissue quickly, but it can drive fat deeper into bone, crack teeth, soften thin areas and separate the sutures of a juvenile skull. Grease trapped by cooking may move back to the surface years later, leaving a yellow, oily and sometimes smelly skull. Community guides consistently treat boiling as the damaging version of this shortcut. [cite:reddit] [cite:jakes]

If speed matters and the skull is a robust adult mammal, use controlled simmering instead:

1. Use a dedicated pot and hold the water at about 71 °C, well below boiling.
2. Keep the skull submerged and begin checking early. The Alaska guide gives roughly 40–60 minutes for fox-, marten- or lynx-sized skulls and up to about eight hours for thawed wolf or bear skulls. Those are useful size examples, not countdown timers. [cite:alaska]
3. Remove the skull as soon as muscle releases easily. Pick off loosened tissue while warm, return it for another short period only if needed, and keep the water from boiling.
4. Flush the braincase and nasal cavity gently. Collect every loose tooth.
5. Expect a longer degreasing stage than after maceration or beetles.

Do not put a greasy bare skull back into very hot water to speed up degreasing. Birds, fish, small mammals and young animals are much safer with beetles or a carefully contained biological method. The Nara preparation guide likewise warns that overheating can deform thin bone. [cite:nara]

</Disclosure>

<Aside title="Ready for degreasing">

No skin, muscle, brain or persistent connective tissue remains. The braincase and nasal cavity have been rinsed, and loose teeth have been recovered. The skull may still be yellow or greasy; that is what the next stage addresses.

</Aside>

## 3. Degreasing {#degreasing}

Bone can look clean while still holding fat. As it dries, grease appears as yellow-brown staining, a translucent patch, a waxy feel or a stale smell. Very fatty animals such as pigs, bears and marine mammals can take months. Smaller and younger animals often finish sooner in my experience, partly because they generally contain less accumulated fat and their more open bone releases it readily.

<Figure asset="oily-spot" />

### Choose a degreaser {#degreaser-comparison}

| Agent                       | Best use                                       | Advantages                                              | Drawbacks                                                                                          |
| --------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Clear dish detergent        | First choice for almost every home preparation | Cheap, easy to obtain, water-based and easy to refresh  | Slow on very fatty skulls; perfumes and dyes can linger                                            |
| Enzymatic laundry detergent | Small remnants of tissue plus grease           | Enzymes can help with both jobs                         | Can damage cartilage and delicate fish or juvenile bone; rinse very well                           |
| Household ammonia           | Persistent grease when detergent has stalled   | Faster alkaline option with a published museum method   | Strong irritating vapour; splash risk; never mix with bleach                                       |
| Acetone                     | Stubborn grease in sound, fully dry bone       | Fast solvent action and reusable when managed correctly | Extremely flammable; specialist container, gloves, ventilation and hazardous-waste disposal needed |

<Disclosure id="detergent" level="3" title="Clear dish detergent: start here">

Use a clear, colourless hand-dishwashing detergent without chlorine bleach. Strong perfume is worth avoiding: bone can retain the smell, and you may forever associate that scent with decomposition. Jake’s Bones makes exactly this practical point. [cite:jakes]

1. Rinse the skull and put it in a plastic tub large enough to submerge it.
2. Add warm water and detergent. I commonly use roughly one part soap to 10–20 parts water, using the stronger end for a greasy skull and a small container. Exact measurement is unnecessary; the bath should feel clearly soapy rather than like ordinary dishwater.
3. Keep the bath warm if practical, but never boiling. A stable warm room or thermostatically controlled water bath works faster than a cold shed.
4. Replace the solution when a greasy film forms, the water becomes heavily cloudy or progress stops. Early baths may need changing every few days; later, every 1-2 weeks may be enough. There's no harm in leaving the skull in the same, safe solution for a very long time.
5. Rinse the skull and let it dry completely every few changes. Wet bone hides grease. If a dry yellow or translucent patch returns, resume with fresh solution.

Cloudy water is a useful sign that material is leaving the skull, but it is not the finish test. The process is finished when the rinsed, fully dry skull has no oily patches or odour and a fresh bath stays mostly clear for about a week. The community guide uses the same combination of a stable bath and dry visual inspection. [cite:reddit]

Enzymatic washing powder can speed work where a little tissue remains, but it also attacks the soft connections that hold delicate skulls together. Use it cautiously on birds, fish, juveniles and very small animals. The National Park Service records damage to fish skeletons from enzyme detergents. [cite:nps]

</Disclosure>

<Disclosure id="ammonia-method" level="3" title="Household ammonia: when detergent stalls">

Use only clear household aqueous ammonia with a known label, outdoors or with strong cross-ventilation. Ammonia vapour irritates the eyes, skin and lungs. Wear splash goggles and gloves specified on the product safety sheet, and never mix ammonia with chlorine bleach. [cite:ammonia]

The National Park Service describes a room-temperature soak made from equal parts regular household ammonia and water. Change it when discoloured, occasionally rinse and dry the skull to inspect for grease, and repeat only while oily areas remain. [cite:nps]

Do not heat an ammonia bath. Keep the labelled container closed when it is not being inspected, away from children, pets, acids and cleaning products. Collect spent solution according to the product label and local household-chemical rules rather than pouring it onto soil or vegetation.

</Disclosure>

<Disclosure id="acetone-method" level="3" title="Acetone: a specialist option for persistent grease">

Acetone dissolves fat quickly and can be reused, but it is a highly flammable solvent with a flash point below ordinary room temperature. Vapour may cause eye and airway irritation, headache or dizziness. Never heat it or work near flames, sparks, switches, motors, heaters, peroxide or smoking. [cite:acetone]

Use pure acetone, not nail-polish remover: remover may contain water, fragrance, oils or dyes that can remain in the bone. Use the original container or a sealable glass or metal vessel explicitly rated for acetone and large enough to hold the dry skull. Many ordinary plastic tubs, bags, seals and coatings soften or leak in acetone.

Work with strong ventilation, splash goggles and gloves rated for acetone. NIOSH lists butyl and other specialist barriers for extended acetone contact; thin latex or ordinary disposable nitrile gloves are not reliable immersion protection. Keep the vessel in secondary containment and label it clearly. [cite:acetone]

Submerge the fully dry skull, close the compatible lid to limit evaporation, and inspect it regularly without introducing water. When the greasy areas have cleared, lift it with compatible tools, let the solvent drain back into the vessel, and allow the skull to air outside until all solvent odour has gone. Store or dispose of used acetone through a household hazardous-waste service; never pour it into a drain or onto the ground.

</Disclosure>

<Aside title="How long does degreasing take?">

- A small, lightly greasy skull may take one or two weeks.
- Many ordinary mammal skulls need several weeks.
- Pig, bear and marine-mammal skulls can need several months, especially if they were boiled.
- Young skulls often finish faster in my experience, but their sutures and separate elements need gentler handling.
- The calendar is secondary: dry the skull, inspect it, and continue if oil returns.

</Aside>

<Aside title="Ready for whitening or assembly">

The skull is fully dry, smells neutral and has no recurring oily or translucent patches. A fresh detergent bath remains mostly clear. If you like the natural bone colour, skip whitening and continue to assembly.

</Aside>

## 4. Whitening is optional {#whitening}

Whitening changes the colour of clean bone; it does not finish defleshing or degreasing. Hydrogen peroxide is the usual choice for a display skull. Sound adult skulls normally tolerate a controlled dilute treatment well, while prolonged or repeated exposure is unnecessary once the colour looks right. Published conservation sources are more cautious where research material, fragile bone or unknown previous treatments are involved. [cite:hendry]

Never use household chlorine bleach. It attacks the organic part of bone and can leave the surface brittle, chalky and permanently weakened. [cite:reddit] [cite:alaska]

### A plain 3% hydrogen peroxide bath {#peroxide-bath}

Plain 3% aqueous peroxide is the easiest concentration for a beginner because it can be used without dilution and the result is gradual. Published skeletal-preparation guidance also uses dilute peroxide for whitening. [cite:harris]

1. Put the clean, degreased skull in a clean plastic tub and add enough peroxide to cover it. Keep antlers and horn sheaths out if you want to retain their natural colour.
2. Cover the tub loosely to exclude dirt while allowing oxygen to escape. Peroxide slowly breaks down into water and oxygen, so a sealed container can pressurise. [cite:icsc]
3. Keep it at room temperature and out of direct sun. Light and heat break peroxide down faster, wasting the active solution before it has finished whitening.
4. Inspect after a few hours and then regularly. Remove the skull when it is slightly darker than your target; it will look paler after drying.
5. Rinse thoroughly with clean water and let it dry slowly. Repeat a short treatment later if you want it whiter.

Wear splash goggles and compatible gloves. Peroxide irritates skin and eyes and is an oxidizer, so keep it away from acetone, fuels, metals and other cleaners. Follow the product label if splashed. [cite:peroxide]

### My diluted cream-developer method {#cream-developer}

I use Fanola 40 Vol Perfumed Cream Developer, labelled 12% hydrogen peroxide. I mix one part developer with roughly four to six parts water. If the stated 12% and volumes are accurate, the starting mixture is approximately 1.7–2.4% peroxide. It does not have to be measured like laboratory work; the important practical point is that I dilute the 12% product substantially. [cite:fanola]

The cream foams much more than plain liquid peroxide, so use a large open tub with generous empty space and place it inside a second tray. Turn the skull if thick foam keeps a surface out of contact. Once the colour is right, rinse every cavity carefully because cream products contain thickeners and other ingredients beyond peroxide and water.

## 5. Teeth, mandibles and final care {#assembly}

Finish every wet stage and let the skull and loose parts dry before gluing. Wet sockets hide poor fits and slow adhesive curing.

<Figure asset="assembly" />

### Replacing teeth {#teeth}

Dry-fit each tooth first. Root shape, wear surface and neighbouring teeth usually reveal the correct socket. If a tooth sits too high, points at an odd angle or will not enter without force, try another socket rather than grinding the root.

I use a small amount of super glue on part of the root. Rehearse the angle, apply one or two small drops to a hidden contact area, seat the tooth and hold it still while it sets. Filling the socket adds mess without improving a good fit.

### Choosing an adhesive {#adhesive-choice}

White PVA glue is slower and easier to reverse with water, which makes it friendly for a first attempt. Paraloid B-72 is a conservation adhesive with better ageing and resolubility, but it requires preparation with solvents. Cyanoacrylate, or super glue, is fast and easy to buy but gives little repositioning time and is difficult to remove cleanly. [cite:adhesives]

| Option              | Best feature                                      | Main tradeoff                                         |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| Keep parts separate | No irreversible decision                          | Small parts need a fitted storage compartment         |
| White PVA glue      | Cheap, slow enough to adjust and water-reversible | Not every formulation ages well                       |
| Paraloid B-72       | Stable and resoluble conservation choice          | Must be obtained and mixed with a solvent             |
| Super glue          | Fast, strong and easy to buy                      | Very little adjustment time; excess is hard to remove |

### Removing a tooth glued into the wrong socket {#removing-glue}

Acetone can swell cured cyanoacrylate and make careful mechanical removal easier. Test a tiny hidden area first. With strong ventilation and eye protection, apply a very small amount around the join with a glass dropper or cotton swab, wait for it to penetrate, then gently test the tooth. Repeat instead of twisting hard. Do not soak an assembled skull unless you intend to degrease the surrounding bone as well. [cite:adhesives]

Acetone may draw grease from the treated spot and change its colour relative to the rest of the skull. Let the area dry completely before regluing. Use the same fire, glove and waste precautions described in the acetone-degreasing section. [cite:acetone]

### Aligning the mandibles {#mandibles}

The left and right lower-jaw halves meet at the mandibular symphysis at the front. Before gluing that join, place both halves against the skull and check that the jaw joints seat correctly and the tooth rows meet naturally.

With badgers, I have found that the jaw joints can trap the condyles. It may be necessary to place the two halves into their joints separately and only then bring the front ends together. If the fit is wrong, stop and check the orientation; forcing the jaws can break the joint or lock them in a crooked position.

Glue the front symphysis if you want the mandible to behave as one piece. Do not glue the moving jaw joints to the skull unless a permanent fixed display is specifically intended.

### Drying, storage and optional records {#storage}

Rinse away the final treatment and let the skull dry slowly at room temperature for several days. Keep it out of direct sun, an oven and direct contact with a radiator. Rapid uneven drying can crack teeth and warp or separate thin bone. [cite:reddit]

Before putting it in a closed box, check the braincase, tooth sockets and nasal cavity for moisture. Store it where temperature and humidity do not swing sharply, support the jaw and delicate projections, and keep loose parts in a small compartment with the skull. Check it again after a few weeks for returning grease, loose teeth, new cracks or insects. [cite:care]

For a personal display, notes are your choice. For a scientific or growing collection, a short record becomes valuable:
